How to improve your Wi-Fi speed

Most home connections are faster than they feel. These are the changes that show up in a measurement, roughly in order of how much they tend to win.

SpeedMeter · updated September 2026

Test on a cable, then test on Wi-Fi in the room where you actually work. The difference between those two numbers is the size of the prize available here — and it is often larger than anything your provider could sell you as an upgrade.

Move the router before changing anything else

Placement beats equipment. Radio waves are absorbed by water, metal and dense material, and they weaken with distance in a way that punishes bad positions severely.

  • Put the router high, central and in the open. A shelf in a hallway beats a cupboard in the corner of the house by a wide margin.
  • Get it away from metal, mirrors, water tanks, aquariums, radiators and concrete. A mirrored wardrobe can cast a genuine radio shadow across a room.
  • Keep it clear of microwaves and cordless phones, which broadcast noise right across the 2.4 GHz band.
  • Do not hide it behind a television. The set itself, its metal stand and its cabling all sit directly in the way.

If the router must stay where the incoming line arrives, run an Ethernet cable to a better spot and put an access point there instead. It is a dull fix, and it works better than anything else on this list.

Use the right band

Modern routers broadcast on two or three bands at once, and they are not interchangeable:

BandSpeedRangeBest for
2.4 GHzLowestLongest, through wallsSmart home devices, far rooms
5 GHzHighMedium, one or two wallsLaptops, phones, TVs — most things
6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7)HighestShortest, same roomA fast device near the router

The catch is that many devices cling to 2.4 GHz once connected, even when a far better 5 GHz signal is available. If your router lets you split the bands into separate network names, doing so and manually joining the 5 GHz one on your main devices is often worth a large jump in a speed test.

Pick a clean channel

In a block of flats you may be sharing the air with thirty other networks. On 2.4 GHz only channels 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap — anything else creates interference for everyone. Most routers choose automatically, but only at boot, so the choice may be months stale and wrong.

Use a Wi-Fi analyser app to see which channels are busy near you, then set yours manually to the quietest of 1, 6 or 11. On 5 GHz there are many more channels; the higher ones (often labelled DFS) are frequently empty because cheaper devices avoid them.

Channel width: wider is not always better

A wider channel carries more data but is more vulnerable to interference. On 5 GHz, 80 MHz is a sensible default and 160 MHz is worth trying if you have a strong signal and little competition. On 2.4 GHz, always use 20 MHz — 40 MHz on that band mostly generates conflict with the neighbours, who then generate it right back.

Mesh or extender?

If a room cannot be reached, you have three options, and they are not equal:

  • Wired access point — an Ethernet run to a second unit. The best result by a distance, at the cost of pulling a cable.
  • Mesh system — several units designed to cooperate, ideally with a dedicated radio for talking to each other. The good compromise, and worth buying as a matched set rather than mixing brands.
  • Single repeater or extender — the cheapest option and usually the worst. A basic extender receives and retransmits on the same radio, halving throughput, and often hands out a second network name that your phone refuses to switch to at the right moment.

Update the firmware, then restart

Router firmware updates fix real performance and security bugs, and many routers do not apply them automatically. Check the admin page. While you are there, restart it — memory leaks and stale state are genuinely common in cheap hardware, and a monthly reboot is not superstition.

Trim what is connected

Every device on the network takes a turn on the air, including ones doing nothing useful. Old phones, idle tablets and a dozen smart plugs all consume airtime. Remove what you do not use, and move the smart-home devices to 2.4 GHz where they will not compete with your laptop.

Quality of Service, used sparingly

Most routers can prioritise certain traffic. Used carefully — prioritising video calls or a games console — it makes a noticeable difference to how the connection feels under load. Used carelessly it caps your throughput for no benefit, so change one setting, measure, and keep it only if the number or the experience improves.

Measure after every change, one at a time. Run the test from the same spot, on the same device, and write the result down. Changing three settings at once tells you nothing about which of them helped.

When the answer is new hardware

If your router predates Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac), no amount of tuning will get you far — the hardware cannot carry modern speeds, and the ISP-supplied box is frequently the weakest link in an otherwise good setup. Wi-Fi 6 is the sensible baseline for a busy household today, and matters most when many devices are active at once rather than for the peak speed of any single one.
