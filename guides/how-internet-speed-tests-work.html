How internet speed tests work

A speed test looks like one number appearing on a dial. Underneath, it is a set of deliberate engineering choices — and knowing them tells you how much to trust the result.

SpeedMeter · updated September 2026

Every speed test does the same four things: time a tiny round trip, pull a lot of data down, push a lot of data up, and watch how steady the timings are. The differences between tests — and between two runs of the same test — come from the details.

Measuring latency first

The test opens with a burst of very small requests, each timed individually. Small matters: the payload must be trivial so that what is measured is the round trip rather than transfer time.

A single sample is unreliable, since one unlucky packet can be delayed by anything. So a series is taken — this site uses fourteen — and the reported ping is the median of the better samples rather than a plain average, which would be dragged upward by a single outlier. Jitter is calculated from the same set, as the average difference between consecutive samples, because that is what it describes: variation, not magnitude.

Why downloads use several connections at once

The most consequential choice in a speed test is how many connections it opens. A single TCP connection is frequently limited by something other than your line — the window size, the latency to the server, a per-connection cap at the far end. On a fast connection with any meaningful distance involved, one stream can report a fraction of what the line can do.

Opening several in parallel — five, here — fills the pipe the way real usage does, since a browser loading a page or a video player buffering ahead both use multiple connections. This is why a test that appears to under-report may simply be measuring one stream, and why parallel tests better reflect what you will actually get.

Discarding the first second

TCP does not begin at full speed. It starts cautiously and accelerates — the mechanism is called slow start — which means the opening moments of any transfer are unrepresentative. Include them and you understate the connection, badly on fast lines where the ramp is a large share of a short test.

The fix is a warm-up window: data still flows, but the first stretch — a second and a half in this test — is excluded from the final average. The gauge shows the live figure throughout, which is why the needle climbs steeply at the start and then settles.

How the final figure is calculated

The honest method is simple: count the bytes that actually arrived after the warm-up, divide by the time they took, convert to megabits. Note the conversion — a byte is eight bits, so a download running at 12.5 MB/s in your browser's download manager is a 100 Mbps connection. The two units being one factor of eight apart explains a great deal of confusion about whether a connection is performing.

The live number on the dial is smoothed, because raw per-instant throughput is spiky and an unsmoothed needle is unreadable. The final result is never smoothed — it comes from raw byte counts and elapsed time.

The upload test is harder than it looks

Uploads are measured the same way in reverse, with one wrinkle: the browser reports progress as data is handed to the operating system, which may be slightly ahead of what has genuinely crossed the network. A well-built test accounts for this by measuring over a long enough window and reconciling against completed transfers, rather than trusting instantaneous progress.

Upload speed deserves more attention than it gets. On many cable and DSL connections it is a small fraction of the download figure, and it is the number that decides whether your video calls look sharp, how long a cloud backup takes, and whether a large email attachment sends promptly.

Why two tests disagree

Differences between tools, or between two runs minutes apart, usually come down to:

  • Server location. A nearby server gives a better result than a distant one. Tests hosted by your own ISP flatter it, because the traffic never leaves their network.
  • Number of connections. As above — single-stream tests report lower figures on fast lines.
  • Test duration. Short tests are noisier and more affected by the ramp-up.
  • Your own device. Browser, processor, and whatever else is running all place a ceiling on what can be measured.
  • The moment. Networks are shared. A neighbour starting a download changes your result.

A few per cent of variation between runs is normal and not worth investigating. Large swings are information: they usually mean interference or congestion rather than a faulty measurement.

Getting a result you can rely on: use a cable, close everything else, disable any VPN, and run the test three times. Take the best figure as your realistic ceiling and the spread between runs as a measure of how stable your connection is.

How much data a test uses

Because a speed test runs for a fixed time rather than transferring a fixed amount, a faster connection moves more data. A full run commonly transfers between 100 MB and 500 MB, and considerably more on a gigabit line. On an uncapped home connection this is irrelevant; on a mobile or metered plan it is worth knowing before you run the test repeatedly.
