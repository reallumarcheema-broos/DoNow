How much internet speed do you actually need?

Providers sell headline numbers. Here is what each activity genuinely requires, and how to add it up for your household without overpaying.

SpeedMeter · updated September 2026

The honest answer for most homes is: less than you are being sold, but with more upload than you are being offered. Here are the real figures.

Two things in that table surprise people. Gaming needs remarkably little bandwidth — a competitive shooter sends tiny packets very frequently, which is why ping and jitter decide your experience and megabits barely register. And video calls are symmetric: the upload requirement equals the download one, which is exactly where cheap plans fall down.

Adding it up for a household

Do not simply sum the peak of everything; not everyone streams 4K simultaneously. A practical method:

  1. Count the people likely to be online at the same time in the evening.
  2. Allow about 25 Mbps each — enough for one 4K stream or a comfortable mix of other things.
  3. Add 50 Mbps of headroom for background updates, backups and smart devices.
  4. Check the upload figure separately: allow 5 Mbps per person who works from home or streams.

A family of four therefore wants roughly 150 Mbps down, with two people working from home pushing the upload requirement to about 10–15 Mbps. That is comfortably met by mid-tier plans almost everywhere — and the jump to a gigabit line, while pleasant for large downloads, will not make streaming or calls any better.

Where more speed genuinely helps

  • Large downloads. A 100 GB game takes about two and a quarter hours at 100 Mbps and roughly thirteen minutes at 1 Gbps. If that is your week, the upgrade is worth it.
  • Many simultaneous heavy users. Student houses, large families, shared offices.
  • Uploading video. Creators pushing large files up feel every megabit of upload.
  • Working with big files in the cloud. Design, video and data work where multi-gigabyte files move both ways.

Where more speed changes nothing

Beyond a comfortable margin, extra bandwidth does not make pages load faster — page load is dominated by latency and by the number of requests, not throughput, past roughly 25 Mbps. It does not improve video quality beyond the stream's own ceiling, does not reduce lag in games, and does not fix a call that stutters because of jitter. If your connection feels slow despite good speed test results, the problem is latency, Wi-Fi or congestion, and a faster plan is money spent on the wrong thing.

Before upgrading: run a test on a cable. If the wired result already matches your current plan, a faster plan will not fix the Wi-Fi problem you actually have — and the Wi-Fi guide is the cheaper place to start.

Do not ignore the upload figure

Cable and DSL connections are usually asymmetric: a plan advertising 500 Mbps down may offer only 20–50 Mbps up, and older lines far less. Full-fibre connections are often symmetric or close to it. If you work from home, back up to the cloud, or share large files, a 200 Mbps symmetric fibre line will serve you better than a 1 Gbps cable line with 30 Mbps of upload — even though the headline number is a fifth of the size.

Checking what you actually use

Rather than guessing, watch your own behaviour. Many routers report per-device usage, and if your busiest evening peaks at 60 Mbps, a 500 Mbps plan is buying headroom you never touch. Run the speed test at a few different times to see what you are currently getting — then decide whether the ceiling, or something inside your home, is what is holding you back.
